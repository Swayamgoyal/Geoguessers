\section{The project in one page}
\paragraph{The question.} Our professor asked us to work like a systems team: take a very large, high-resolution
DEM (Himalaya, 90\,m down to 1\,m), use only a laptop, and find out (a) where today's standard tools (GDAL, QGIS)
stop being practical and \emph{why}: what is the real bottleneck (disk, RAM, CPU, GPU transfer, rendering)? And (b)
how to process and display only what the user is looking at, using a \textbf{pyramid} (the same DEM stored at several
resolutions), computing slope / aspect / hillshade \textbf{on demand}, and using the CPU and GPU where each really
helps. Our literature review turned this into these research gaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{G1}] No study reports where standard GDAL/QGIS terrain tools stop being practical on one laptop,
  separated into stages (read, compute, CPU$\leftrightarrow$GPU transfer, write, display).
  \item[\textbf{G2}] No study computes terrain derivatives on demand from pyramid levels, or measures how wrong a
  coarse-level slope is against the time it saves.
  \item[\textbf{G3}] (stretch) Accuracy of fast approximate viewshed (XDraw) vs exact R3 in high relief; A* vs Dijkstra.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What is finished.} Phase 0 (setup), Phase 1 (data), Phase 2 (baseline of the standard tools, E1/E2/QGIS),
Phase 3 (our own CPU and GPU engine, E3/E4), Phase 4 (derivatives on demand from the pyramid, E5), all audited number
by number against the raw data. Phase 5: the interactive viewer (a tile server, a browser viewer with continuous
debounced pan/zoom and neighbour prefetch, a QGIS plugin), built and measured in E6 (1{,}068 runs). What remains are the
final deliverables: final report, slides, demo.

\paragraph{The answer, in seven lines.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \code{gdaldem} (GDAL's terrain tool, which QGIS also calls) \textbf{never runs out of memory}: it handles
  10.8 billion cells (43\,GB) with 0.87\,GB of RAM. It just gets slower: 3.5\,min for one slope pass at 1\,m.
  \item Its bottleneck is \textbf{computation on a single CPU thread} (73\% of the time; 1 of 22 threads used).
  \item For \textbf{display}, the bottleneck is \textbf{reading full-resolution data}: without a pyramid QGIS needs
  32\,s to draw the whole 1\,m area; with a pyramid 0.09\,s at any size.
  \item \textbf{Our CPU engine} computes slope \textbf{2.1--3.5$\times$ faster} than \code{gdaldem} on the same cores
  (1\,m: 60\,s vs 208\,s), identical results, memory flat at $\sim$1.4\,GB. Loading the whole grid into RAM (the naive
  way) breaks above roughly 0.5--0.7 billion cells.
  \item \textbf{Our GPU kernel} computes 50$\times$ faster than the CPU kernel, but the run is only 1.0--1.4$\times$
  faster (1\,m: 44\,s): 95\% of the GPU's own time is copying, and the run is limited by decompressing the input and
  writing the output. \textbf{The bottleneck moved from computation to data movement.}
  \item \textbf{On demand from the pyramid}, one display tile costs 5--8\,ms at every zoom level, 8--450$\times$ less
  than computing it exactly at full resolution. The price: slope comes out \emph{too low}, by 2.3$^\circ$ RMSE one
  level up to 6.4$^\circ$ four levels up (worst on steep slopes). Aspect is barely affected.
  \item \textbf{Our viewer} computes every $512^2$ tile on demand while the user pans and zooms continuously. With
  neighbour prefetch 98--100\% of the tiles entering the view while panning are already in the browser; a 150\,ms
  trailing debounce cuts the server's tiles per zoom gesture from 62 to 19 (3.3$\times$); prefetch does not slow the
  visible tiles. The remaining latency comes from decoding blocks and the browser's 6 connections, not from computing.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where we go next.} The final deliverables (the final plan's Phase 6, Section~\ref{sec:future}): final
report, slides and a recorded demo of the viewer and the QGIS plugin as a fallback for the live demo.

\section{Concepts you need (glossary)}
\begin{longtable}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.4cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{12.0cm}}

\endhead
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Data and formats}} \\
DEM & Digital Elevation Model: a grid (raster) where each cell stores a ground height. \\
Resolution vs cells & Halving the cell size quadruples the number of cells. Our area at 8\,m is 169\,M cells; at 2\,m
2.7\,B; at 1\,m 10.8\,B. Run time follows the number of cells, which is why we talk in cells, not metres. \\
float32 / float64 & 4 / 8 bytes per number. 10.8\,B cells $\times$ 4\,B = 43\,GB, 2.6$\times$ our 16\,GB RAM. float32 is
what the files store; float64 is more precise arithmetic. \\
MB vs MiB & MB = $10^6$ bytes, MiB = $2^{20}$ (5\% more). Our harness records MiB; our documents use MB/GB. GPU memory
is given in MiB/GiB as the driver reports it. (Mixing these was one of the audit fixes.) \\
CRS, UTM 44N & Coordinate reference system. Slope needs the cell size in metres, so everything is reprojected to UTM
zone 44N (EPSG:32644). Degrees would give wrong slopes. \\
Resampling & Moving a grid onto a new grid. \emph{Nearest} copies the closest value (keeps values, blocky),
\emph{bilinear}/\emph{cubic} blend neighbours (smooth), \emph{average} averages all cells that fall into a coarser cell
(used to build pyramids). \\
GeoTIFF, strips, tiles & The file format. Data stored in rows (strips) or squares (tiles, e.g.\ 512$\times$512). Tiles
let a program read a small window without reading whole rows. \\
Compression, predictor & LZW, DEFLATE, ZSTD shrink the file; the price is CPU time to decompress on every read. The
floating-point predictor (=3) makes height data compress much better. \\
COG & Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFF: a tiled GeoTIFF with its pyramid stored inside. All our prepared grids are COGs
(512 tiles, ZSTD). \\
Overview / pyramid level & A coarser copy stored inside the file. Level $k$ has cell size $\approx 2^k \times$ base
(2\,m $\to$ 4, 8, 16\,\dots\,m). GDAL halves the size and rounds down, so when the grid size is not divisible by $2^k$
a level cell is slightly bigger than $2^k$ fine cells (8.002 instead of 8). \\
Vertical datum & What height zero means. HMA 8\,m and Chamoli 2\,m use the WGS84 ellipsoid; Copernicus the EGM2008
geoid: about 24.5--26\,m apart here. A constant offset does not change slope, but matters for viewsheds. \\
NMAD & Normalised median absolute deviation: a robust standard deviation used to compare DEMs. \\
Synthetic grid & Our 2\,m and 1\,m grids of the whole area are \emph{cubic upsamples} of the 8\,m DEM. They have the right
size but no real extra detail: used for timing only, never accuracy. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Terrain operators}} \\
3$\times$3 operators & Slope, aspect, hillshade, TRI, TPI, roughness each look at a cell and its 8 neighbours. One
visit per cell: O(N). Faster versions differ in implementation, not in the maths. \\
Horn vs ZT & Two ways to estimate the height gradient (Section~\ref{sec:formulas}). Horn uses all 8 neighbours with
weights; Zevenbergen--Thorne only the 4 direct ones. GDAL's default is Horn. \\
Slope / aspect / hillshade & Steepness in degrees; direction the slope faces (0--360$^\circ$ from north); shaded relief
lit by a sun at azimuth 315$^\circ$, altitude 45$^\circ$ (grey value 1--255, 0 = nodata). \\
TRI, TPI, roughness & Terrain Ruggedness Index (Riley: root of summed squared differences; Wilson: mean absolute
difference), Topographic Position Index (cell minus mean of neighbours), max $-$ min of the window. \\
Nodata & Cells without a value ($-9999$). An operator output is nodata if any of the 9 window cells is nodata;
\code{gdaldem} also leaves the outer ring of the grid empty. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Measurement}} \\
File cache, cold / warm & Windows keeps recently read file data in spare RAM (the standby list). Cold: we empty it
first, so the file must come from disk. Warm: immediate repeat, data may come from RAM. \\
Peak working set & The most RAM a process held at any moment: our ``peak memory''. \\
ns per cell & Run time / number of cells, in nanoseconds: makes grids of different sizes comparable. \\
Median, IQR, spread & We repeat runs and report the median (robust to one odd run). IQR = 75th $-$ 25th percentile;
spread = (max $-$ min) / median. \\
Warm-up run & rep 0: a first run that is thrown away (loads programs and caches). \\
P-, E-, LP-cores & Our CPU has 6 fast performance cores (12 threads), 8 slower efficiency and 2 very slow low-power cores.
Where Windows puts a process changes its speed by up to 2.7$\times$; we pin tools to the P-cores. \\
Turbo budget & Cores share a power/heat budget: if other cores are busy, the active core clocks lower. \\
Defender scan, \code{\$Deleted} & Windows Defender scans new files; a file deleted while being scanned keeps its space
reserved in a hidden NTFS area until the scan ends. \\
Stage split & Dividing a run into read / compute / write (or copy / kernel for the GPU) to see where time goes. \\
Amdahl's law & If a part $p$ of a run is sped up infinitely, the whole run speeds up at most $1/(1-p)$. Read + write =
27\% of gdaldem $\Rightarrow$ parallelising only compute gives at most $\sim$3.7$\times$. \\
Read-bound / write-bound & The run's speed is set by reading (decompressing) input or writing output, not by
computation. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Our engine (CPU)}} \\
Window, halo & The grid is processed in 2048$\times$2048 windows; each is read with a 1-cell border (halo) so the
3$\times$3 operators are exact at window edges. \\
Pipeline & Three stages that overlap: reader threads decode windows, the main thread computes, one writer thread
writes. Bounded queues (at most 6 windows in flight) keep memory flat. \\
Numba, JIT, \code{prange} & Numba compiles a Python loop to machine code just in time; \code{prange} splits the rows
over all cores. The compiled kernel is cached on disk. \\
Fused kernel & Slope, aspect and hillshade computed from one gradient pass: three products for little more than one. \\
Naive version & Read the whole grid into memory and compute with NumPy: simple, but needs $\sim$24 bytes per cell. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Our engine (GPU)}} \\
CUDA kernel, CuPy & A function run by thousands of GPU threads at once (one per cell). We write the kernels in CUDA C
and compile them through CuPy at run time. \\
H2D / D2H, PCIe & Copies host$\to$device and device$\to$host over the PCIe bus (here 4.0 $\times$8). Moving a cell in and
out costs $\sim$0.9\,ns; computing it 0.04\,ns. \\
Pinned memory & Page-locked host memory: copies run at full PCIe speed and can overlap with kernels. \\
CUDA streams & Independent queues of GPU work; with 2 streams the copies of one window overlap the kernel of the next. \\
Crossover & The window size above which the GPU (including copies) beats the CPU: $512^2$ cells for slope. \\
VRAM, TDR & GPU memory (8\,GiB, we capped use at 4\,GiB); TDR is Windows' 2\,s GPU watchdog (our kernels take ms). \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 4 (pyramid)}} \\
On demand & Compute the derivative from the pyramid level being displayed, at that level's cell size. \\
Truth (E5) & Slope computed at full resolution, then averaged over exactly the fine cells a coarse cell covers. \\
Footprint & The fine cells covered by one coarse cell (those whose centres lie inside it). \\
Bias, RMSE, MAE & Mean of (on demand $-$ truth); root mean squared error; mean absolute error. Negative bias = too low. \\
Fully valid cell & A coarse cell whose footprint and 8 neighbours contain no nodata: the only cells we score. \\
Circular mean, $R$ & Average of directions (via sin/cos); $R$ (0--1) says how much the directions agree. \\
Levels up & How many pyramid levels above the source data: the error depends on this, not on metres. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 5: the tile server}} \\
Tile lattice & A fixed grid of $512\times512$ tiles per pyramid level, aligned to that level's own $512^2$ COG blocks.
A tile is named (level, $t_x$, $t_y$); with its 1-cell halo it touches up to 9 stored blocks. \\
Level choice, hysteresis & The viewer shows the finest level whose cell is at most one screen pixel (real cell sizes,
not $2^k$). Hysteresis ($\pm$10\%) keeps it from flipping between two levels when the zoom sits near a boundary. \\
Foreground / background (fg / bg) & Foreground tiles are what the user is looking at now; background tiles are
speculative prefetch. They have separate queues and separate worker threads. \\
GIL, \code{nogil} & Python's global interpreter lock lets only one thread run Python code at a time. Numba functions
compiled with \code{nogil=True} (and zlib compression) release it, so several tiles really run in parallel on
several threads. \\
Palette PNG, Z\_RLE & An 8-bit PNG storing one byte per cell plus a 256-colour palette (index 0 transparent =
nodata): smaller and faster to encode than RGBA. Z\_RLE is zlib's run-length deflate mode: 2--3$\times$ faster than
default deflate for 5--15\% larger tiles. \\
Block cache, result cache & Two server caches: decoded $512^2$ blocks as float32 (1024\,MiB; all-nodata blocks
stored as a 0-byte marker) and finished PNG tiles (512\,MiB), keyed by a BLAKE2b hash of the full tile
specification. \\
SLRU & Segmented LRU cache: new entries go to a probation segment (20\%); an entry hit again moves to the protected
segment (80\%). A one-off scan (a prefetch ring never visited) cannot flush entries that are really reused. \\
Single-flight & Identical concurrent requests share one computation: 100 identical requests compute once. \\
Generation & A counter per viewer session, raised at every zoom commit. Work for an older generation is superseded
(dropped) on the server. \\
Cancel token & A flag each job checks between stages (before dequeue, after each block read, before the kernel,
before encoding); a cancelled job stops at the next check. \\
Promotion & A foreground request for a tile still queued as prefetch moves that job to the foreground queue. \\
Yielding (prefetch) & A background worker does not start a new tile while any foreground tile is queued or running
(\code{DEMLAB\_BG\_YIELD}, default on). \\
Memory guard, 503 & Prefetch pauses when free RAM falls below 1.0\,GB (\code{DEMLAB\_BG\_MIN\_FREE\_GB}); while
paused, prefetch requests get HTTP 503 at once and the client backs off. \\
Efficiency class, last-level cache & Windows labels every logical CPU with an efficiency class; on our CPU the E-
and LP-cores get the same class, so we tell them apart by which last-level cache they sit behind. \\
Pinning (threads) & Fixing a thread to chosen CPUs (\code{SetThreadAffinityMask}): foreground workers on the 12
P-core threads (CPUs 0, 1, 10--19), prefetch workers on the 8 E-cores (CPUs 2--9); LP-cores (20--21) unused. \\
\code{Server-Timing} & An HTTP response header; every tile reports its queue, read, decode, kernel, colour-map and
encode times and its cache tier in it. \\
Event-driven disconnect & The server learns that a browser aborted a request from the connection-closed event,
instead of checking every 20\,ms, and cancels the job. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 5: the viewer}} \\
Commit & The moment the viewer fixes a new view (level and extent) and requests its tiles. \\
Debounce, trailing debounce & Waiting until input has stopped before acting. Trailing: the zoom commits once,
150\,ms after the \emph{last} wheel tick, instead of on every tick. Meanwhile the screen shows the tiles it already
has, rescaled. \\
\code{maxWaitMs} & An optional forced commit after continuous scrolling for that long; off (measured to nearly double
the tiles). \\
Fallback chain & What a frame draws for a missing tile: a coarser cached level, else a finer cached level, else the
overview (the coarsest level, always loaded). So the view is never blank. \\
Prefetch ring & After a commit, the 8 view-sized rectangles around the view (N, S, E, W and the corners) are loaded at
background priority, at most 2 requests at a time, aborted when the user goes elsewhere. \\
Velocity-first & Ring order: the direction of the last pan first, then cardinals before corners, then nearer before
farther. \\
Recentre & The ring is rebuilt when the camera moves more than 25\% of the view off the committed centre (at most
once per 100\,ms); ring requests no longer needed are aborted. \\
Back-off & The client stops prefetch for 2\,s when the p95 of its last 20 foreground tiles exceeds 250\,ms (or on a
503). \\
6 connections per origin & A browser opens at most 6 HTTP/1.1 connections to one server; further tile requests wait
for a free one. \\
\code{AbortController} & The browser mechanism that cancels a fetch; it closes the connection, which the server
sees as a disconnect. \\
Box zoom, \code{POST /view} & Shift + drag zooms the viewer to a box. \code{POST /view} computes a box directly as a PNG
or GeoTIFF; the QGIS plugin uses it. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 5: measuring the viewer (E6)}} \\
Virtual client & A Python port of the viewer's policy (camera, level choice, browser cache, debounce, generations,
aborts, prefetch) that replays a trace in real time against the live server with 6 connections and 60\,Hz frames. \\
Trace & A seeded, stored script of user input (pans, wheel bursts, notches, box zooms) replayed identically in every
run. \\
Notched wheel & A mouse wheel that clicks in discrete steps 30--300\,ms apart, unlike the 17\,ms gaps of a 60\,Hz
burst. \\
Pan hit rate & Share of tiles already cached in the browser when they first enter the view during a pan. \\
Prefetch precision & Prefetched tiles later drawn, divided by all prefetched tiles (0.34 means about 3 fetched per 1
used). \\
TTFF, complete & Time to first frame: from the last wheel tick (or the end of a box animation) to the first frame
showing a tile of the committed level. \emph{Complete}: to the first frame with every visible tile of that level. \\
Blank area-time & Share of view area $\times$ frames not drawn from the needed level's own tiles (a preview or the
overview instead). \\
Server tiles per gesture & Foreground jobs the server finished or cancelled mid-render, divided by zoom gestures. \\
p50 / p95 & The median and 95th percentile of tile latencies: 95\% of tiles are faster than the p95. \\
Cold start & Every E6 run starts a new server with empty caches, so latencies are on the slow side. \\
Chrome DevTools protocol (CDP) & The interface through which a program drives Chrome. We drive headless Chrome over it
in real time with the \code{websockets} package (no Playwright). \emph{Virtual time} (a headless mode in which the page's
clock does not follow real time) distorted every network wait and was dropped. \\
High-resolution event loop & Python 3.12's default event-loop clock on Windows moves in 15.6\,ms steps; the virtual
client runs its own loop on the high-resolution clock (timers accurate to about 1\,ms). \\
RSS & Resident set size: the RAM a process holds (server memory in E6, in MiB). \\
\midrule

E1--E6 & E1 gdaldem baseline; E2 storage layouts; E1q QGIS display; E3 CPU engine; E4 GPU engine; E5 pyramid accuracy
and time; E6 interactive viewer (Phase 5: virtual client replaying traces against the tile server, 1{,}068 runs). \\

\subsection{The terrain operators and their formulas}\label{sec:formulas}
Label the 3$\times$3 window around cell $e$ as
\[
\begin{array}{ccc} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i \end{array}
\qquad \text{cell size } \Delta x = \Delta y .
\]
\textbf{Horn (1981):}
\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{(c+2f+i)-(a+2d+g)}{8\Delta x},\qquad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{(a+2b+c)-(g+2h+i)}{8\Delta y}. \]
\textbf{Zevenbergen--Thorne (1987):}
\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{f-d}{2\Delta x},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{b-h}{2\Delta y}. \]
Then
\[ s = \arctan\sqrt{(\partial z/\partial x)^2 + (\partial z/\partial y)^2}, \qquad
\text{aspect} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(-\tfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}, -\tfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right) \]
(\textbf{slope} $s$; \textbf{aspect} is the direction of steepest descent in degrees from north, nodata on perfectly flat
cells); \textbf{hillshade} (sun azimuth $\phi$, altitude $\theta$):
$\cos\gamma = \sin\theta\cos s + \cos\theta\sin s\cos(\phi - \text{aspect})$, value $= 1 + 254\max(\cos\gamma, 0)$, as
\code{gdaldem} does; \textbf{TRI} (Riley) $= \sqrt{\sum (z_n - z_e)^2}$, (Wilson) the mean of $|z_n - z_e|$ over the 8
neighbours $n$; \textbf{TPI} $= z_e - \text{mean}(z_n)$; \textbf{roughness} $= \max - \min$ of the window.

Three consequences used later: (1) every operator divides by the cell size, so slope computed on a coarser pyramid
level is \emph{different} (smoother), not just faster: that is what E5 measures. (2) All six need the same few
neighbour differences, so computing them together (one pass) shares most of the work. (3) \code{gdaldem} adds the
neighbour values in float32; our float64 engine therefore differs from it by tiny rounding on nearly flat cells.

\subsection{Error measures used in Phase 4}
For the $n$ scored cells with on-demand value $c_j$ and truth $t_j$:
$\text{bias} = \frac1n\sum (c_j - t_j)$, $\text{MAE} = \frac1n\sum |c_j - t_j|$,
$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac1n\sum (c_j - t_j)^2}$. Aspect differences are taken around the circle
($\min(|\Delta|, 360-|\Delta|)$).

\section{What we did, in order}
\subsection{Phases 0--2 (setup, data, baseline)}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Setup.} Python environment \code{geo} (GDAL 3.13.3, rasterio, Numba, CuPy 14.2, FastAPI) via Miniforge at
\code{D:\textbackslash miniforge3} (the default path failed because our user folder name has a space), QGIS 3.44.14.
Checked: CuPy runs on the RTX 4060; QGIS slope matched an OpenTopography reference to $10^{-5}$ degrees.
\item \textbf{Choosing data.} The first downloads (an Everest 90\,m crop, two single 8\,m strips) were too small or
patchy. We switched to the NASA High Mountain Asia 8\,m \emph{mosaic} tile-571 (Uttarakhand), downloaded Copernicus
30\,m and 90\,m for the same box through the OpenTopography API, and found a free real 2\,m DEM of the Chamoli valley
inside the tile (Bhushan \& Shean, Zenodo).
\item \textbf{One area, many resolutions.} Run time depends on the number of cells, not on which mountain it is. One
area means grid size is the only thing that changes, and the real 2\,m data gives a ``truth'' for accuracy.
\item \textbf{Checking the data.} Tile-571 is 90.4\% valid (gaps in the south-east). The 8\,m tile is 24.5\,m below
Copernicus (datum). Chamoli 2\,m averaged to 8\,m matches the 8\,m DEM to a median of $-0.7$\,m (NMAD 5.1\,m). Chamoli
was re-gridded with nearest-neighbour (only 0.09/0.16\,m off our grid), so its heights are untouched.
\item \textbf{Are synthetic grids OK?} No free 1\,m DEM exists here. On a 16\,M-cell crop, an 8\,m grid rebuilt from
16\,m takes the same gdaldem time as the real one, but its slopes are off by 2.3--3.0$^\circ$ RMSE, and nearest-neighbour
data compresses 26\% better (fake fast reads). Decision: synthetic 2\,m and 1\,m, \emph{cubic}, timing only.
\item \textbf{Building the harness} (Section~\ref{sec:harness}): exact per-process measurements, cold-cache flushing,
resumable sweeps.
\item \textbf{First E1 pass} (74 min, 560 runs, 0 failures), then a critical review: the ``read'' reference was wrong,
one run was a 2$\times$ outlier, conditions were not recorded.
\item \textbf{Fixing the method.} Core test $\to$ pin to performance cores. New pure read reference. Defender's CPU use
measured (tiny). Run conditions saved with every sweep.
\item \textbf{Second attempt} ran into a full disk: 108\,GB of deleted outputs held in \code{\$Deleted} while Defender
scanned them. We stopped, freed space, added a safeguard (wait until deleted space is released), re-ran.

\section{How the measurements work}\label{sec:harness}
\subsection{One run}
\code{bench/harness.py} starts the tool (e.g.\ \code{gdaldem slope in.tif out.tif}, or \code{python -m demlab.cli \dots}
for our engine) as its own process, pinned to a set of cores, and waits. When it ends, the harness reads from Windows,
for that process only: wall time, peak memory, total CPU time (so CPU time / wall time = average cores used), bytes
read and written. It also counts bytes physically read from the disks: if the disk read much more than the process
did, someone else was using the disk (our ``foreign disk read'' interference signal). Our engine additionally prints its
own stage timings (read wait, compute, write wait, writer busy; GPU copy/kernel times) as JSON, which go into the
\code{extra} column. One row per run in \code{results/runs.csv}. An early version sampled counters every 50\,ms and missed
the end of short runs; reading exact totals from the finished process fixed that.

\subsection{Cold cache}
Emptying the Windows file cache needs admin rights, so a scheduled task (\code{GeoGuessers-FlushCache} in the
\code{\textbackslash GeoGuessers} folder of the Task Scheduler) runs Sysinternals \code{RAMMap -Et} with admin rights; the benchmark triggers it without being admin. Verified: after a flush the whole file is read from disk again.
(An early mistake used \code{-Ec}, which is not a RAMMap option.)

\subsection{Protocol}
Real grids: 5 cold runs (cache emptied before each), then 1 warm-up and 5 warm runs. Synthetic 2\,m/1\,m: 3 cold + 1
warm (saved $\sim$5 hours; operator comparisons do not depend on size, so they come from the real grids). Medians are
reported; median and IQR of every configuration are in \code{results/run\_stats.md} (median IQR 1.2--1.6\% of the
median for E1--E4). Plugged in, Balanced power plan, Defender on (only scratch folders excluded), recorded per sweep in
\code{results/env/}.

\subsection{Why we pin to performance cores}
The same 8\,m slope job pinned to each of the 22 logical CPUs: performance cores 3.7--4.1\,s, efficiency cores
$\sim$6.6\,s (1.7$\times$), low-power cores up to 10.5\,s (2.7$\times$). Unpinned, Windows may move a background process
onto slow cores mid-run (one first-pass run took 108.9\,s instead of $\sim$56\,s). Pinning runs every baseline tool at its
best, so our speed-ups are \emph{conservative}. Our engine was measured both on the same 12 P-core threads (fair
comparison) and on all 22.

\subsection{Why only two folders are excluded from Defender}
Defender's CPU use during a run is tiny (0.02--0.05 core). The problem is \emph{after} the run: it scans each new
10--40\,GB output, and a file deleted meanwhile keeps its space reserved until the scan ends. That filled the disk once
(108\,GB) and slowed later runs. Excluding just our two scratch folders loses no protection. The harness also waits until
deleted space is released before the next run and stops cleanly if that takes more than 15 minutes.

\subsection{Splitting a gdaldem run into stages}
Two reference runs per grid: \textbf{read} (\code{bench/read\_blocks.py}: read and decompress every block, nothing else)
and \textbf{copy} (\code{gdal\_translate} to an uncompressed GeoTIFF: same read plus same-size write). Then write + tool
overhead $\approx$ copy $-$ read, and compute $\approx$ gdaldem $-$ copy. (The first read reference, \code{gdalinfo
-checksum}, spent half its time computing the checksum.)

\subsection{GPU safety}
Our rule was not to put the graphics card or the laptop at risk. So: CUDA kernels only through CuPy (no clock,
power-limit, fan or driver changes); GPU memory capped at 4\,GiB; before every GPU run the harness reads the GPU
temperature and waits until it is below 75\,$^\circ$C, and stops the sweep if a run ends at 85\,$^\circ$C or more; every
kernel is milliseconds, far below the 2\,s Windows watchdog. Result: max 63\,$^\circ$C, median 58\,$^\circ$C, max 2.0\,GiB.

\section{Phase 2 results: the standard tools}
\subsection{E1: how gdaldem scales}
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_scaling.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_memory.png}
\caption{Left: run time vs cells. Right: peak memory vs cells.}
\end{figure}
\textbf{Left chart.} Both axes logarithmic; a straight 45$^\circ$ line means ``time proportional to cells''. Slope and
hillshade are straight from 12\,M cells up: \textbf{linear scaling}, about 19--25\,ns per cell. At 90\,m the line bends
because a run lasts 0.07\,s, mostly program start-up. Slope: 4.0\,s (8\,m), 6.6\,s (2\,m real), 54\,s (2\,m synthetic),
208\,s (1\,m).

\textbf{Right chart.} gdaldem's memory \emph{stops growing} at $\sim$0.87\,GB while the grid grows past the 16\,GB line:
gdaldem reads a few rows, computes, writes, moves on (streaming), keeping only GDAL's block cache (5\% of RAM) plus the
program. \textbf{Meaning:} gdaldem cannot ``break'' on memory here. ``Where does it break?'' has the answer ``it doesn't;
it becomes slow''. The real breaking points are memory-hungry approaches (Phase 3's naive version) and display.

\textbf{Cold vs warm} differ by 2\% or less: reading is a small part of the run on this NVMe SSD (cold reads measured at
2.7--3.3\,GB/s), and at 2\,m/1\,m even warm runs read 3.8/14.2\,GB from disk (at 1\,m the file exceeds the cache; at 2\,m
gdaldem's own $\sim$11\,GB output pushes the input out). Our pilot (lit review) saw 3$\times$; it was timed through QGIS on
another laptop, so its first runs most likely included loading the programs.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_stages.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_operators.png}
\caption{Left: stage split of a gdaldem slope run. Right: cost per cell of the ten configurations (2\,m real).}
\end{figure}
\textbf{Stage split.} At every real size $\sim$15\% is reading + decompressing, 11--14\% writing, \textbf{71--74\%
computing}. This contradicts the common claim that data movement dominates: for gdaldem on a fast SSD it is the
arithmetic on one thread. Amdahl: parallelising only the computation gives at most $\sim$3.7$\times$; going further
needs parallel decompression and less writing.

\textbf{Operators.} All ten cost 17.5--27\,ns per cell; Horn vs ZT differ by at most 6\%. The formula is not where
speed comes from; implementation and data movement are. \textbf{Pinning:} on 2\,m synthetic, unpinned cold runs ranged
55.8--108.9\,s (74\% spread); pinned 53.7--55.2\,s (2.8\%).

\subsection{E2: does the file layout matter?}
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{e2_storage.png}
\caption{File size, read time and gdaldem slope time per storage layout (2\,m synthetic, 2.7\,B cells).}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Size:} uncompressed 10.8--10.9\,GB; LZW 5.0\,GB; DEFLATE 3.9\,GB; ZSTD 3.8\,GB (2.9$\times$ smaller).
  \item \textbf{Read:} uncompressed 3.3--4.0\,s; ZSTD 7.8\,s; DEFLATE 11.4\,s; LZW 20.1\,s (decompression costs CPU).
  \item \textbf{gdaldem slope:} uncompressed strips 47\,s (fastest), ZSTD 54\,s, DEFLATE 57\,s, LZW 66\,s (+39\%), because
  gdaldem decompresses inside its single thread.
  \item \textbf{Write:} compressed files take 6--9$\times$ longer to create (ZSTD 124\,s vs 14\,s): a one-time cost.
  \item \textbf{Tiles vs strips:} strips are fine for a row-by-row pass; tiles matter for reading small windows (the
  viewer); 512 slightly better than 256.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Meaning:} layout changes gdaldem time by up to $\sim$40\%. Tiled ZSTD (our COGs) is the right compressed format;
avoid LZW.

\subsection{QGIS: why the pyramid matters}
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.58\linewidth]{e1q_render.png}
\caption{QGIS: time to draw one 1920$\times$1080 view.}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Whole area, with pyramid:} 0.09\,s at 8\,m, 2\,m and 1\,m (peak 0.21\,GB): QGIS reads only the level whose
  cells match the screen pixels.
  \item \textbf{Whole area, no pyramid:} 0.79\,s, 8.8\,s, \textbf{31.9\,s} (peak $\sim$1.03\,GB): it decompresses the whole
  full-resolution level (about 12.7\,GB at 1\,m), close to the 29\,s a pure read takes. 360$\times$ slower at 1\,m.
  \item \textbf{Zoomed in:} $\sim$0.1\,s with or without pyramid (only the window is read). \textbf{Opening:} 0.02--0.03\,s.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Meaning:} the display bottleneck is how much full-resolution data a view must read. A pyramid makes zoomed-out
views cost the same at any grid size, which is the meeting's proposed design.

\key{Phase 2 answer to G1: on this laptop the standard tools do not fail at 1\,m, they stop being practical.
Processing is limited by single-threaded computation; display by reading full-resolution data without a pyramid.}

cannot help any more; reading and writing less can, which is exactly what on-demand display does.}

\subsection{What changed and how the work was organised}

\paragraph{What Phases 0--4 told the design} (from measurements, not assumptions):
\begin{itemize}
  \item A $512^2$ tile costs 5--8\,ms on demand, of which the slope kernel is $\sim$0.5\,ms. \textbf{Decoding, not
  computing, is the cost.}
  \item Every pyramid level is stored in $512\times512$ compressed blocks. A $512^2$ window with a 1-cell halo decodes
  up to \textbf{9 blocks}; a shared cache of decoded blocks can bring that to about 1 per tile.
  \item 12 performance-core threads beat all 22; efficiency cores are 1.7$\times$ slower and slow the foreground when
  mixed in.
  \item Exact (full-resolution) tiles cost up to 2.9\,s at 32\,m: never prefetch them.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The goals (from the spec).} (1) Continuous pan and zoom over a fixed $512^2$ tile lattice per level, box
zoom kept as Shift + drag and \code{POST /view}. (2) Debounced zoom: commit once after the last wheel tick; during the
gesture show existing tiles rescaled, never a blank area. (3) Speculative prefetch of the 8 neighbouring views at low
priority on the efficiency cores, cancelled when the user moves elsewhere. (4) Caches at every tier. (5) Cancellation
by generation. (6) Bounded memory (server $\approx$2.2\,GB budget). (7) Exact mode without prefetch; the expected E5
error of the shown level displayed. (8) E6 with two gates: \textbf{gate 1} prefetch raises foreground p95 by at most
10\%; \textbf{gate 2} the debounce cuts server tiles per zoom gesture while the first frame arrives $<$100\,ms after the
debounce.

\subsection{Tile engine core (final plan Phase 1)}\label{sec:p5engine}
Files: \code{demlab/view.py}, \code{kernels/tile\_k.py}, \code{blockcache.py}, \code{resultcache.py}, \code{slru.py},
\code{colormap.py}, \code{cancel.py}. Design and why:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$512^2$ lattice aligned to the COG blocks.} Every level is stored in 512 blocks (checked), so one tile =
  one stored block plus its 1-cell halo. Each level's lattice comes from its own geotransform and size (GDAL's levels
  are not exact halvings, Phase 4). Edge and partial tiles are filled with nodata (transparent); tiles outside the
  DEM are answered as empty.
  \item \textbf{Level choice:} the finest level whose cell is $\leq$ one device pixel, using real cell sizes (not
  $2^k$), with $\pm$10\% hysteresis. The browser mirrors the same rule (tested on a shared fixture).
  \item \textbf{GIL-free per-tile kernels.} One tile is rendered on one thread by separate single-threaded
  \code{nogil} Numba kernels (slope / aspect / hillshade, TRI, TPI, roughness), so many tiles run in parallel on many
  threads. The spec suggested recompiling the existing kernels; we added separate ones so the parallel engine kernels
  stay untouched and E3/E4 stay reproducible. One Numba and one GDAL thread per tile; fixed pools (no oversubscription).
  \item \textbf{Block cache.} Our reader reads whole aligned blocks, decodes each once (single-flight), keeps them as
  float32 in a byte-budgeted SLRU (1024\,MiB) and assembles windows from them. All-nodata blocks are a 0-byte marker.
  This removes the halo re-decoding found in E3.
  \item \textbf{Result cache + single-flight.} Finished PNGs (512\,MiB, SLRU), keyed by a BLAKE2b hash of the canonical
  tile specification (dataset, level, tile, operator and every parameter); 100 identical concurrent requests
  compute once.
  \item \textbf{Palette PNG.} The colour map has 256 entries, so a tile is one byte per cell plus a palette (index 0
  transparent = nodata), smaller and faster to encode than RGBA. The quantiser is a \code{nogil} Numba function and
  the PNG is written by our own small writer with \code{zlib} in run-length mode (Z\_RLE): 2--3$\times$ faster than
  default deflate for 5--15\% larger tiles. Both release the GIL.
  \item \textbf{Modes:} \emph{auto} (the level's own cells, E5 error), \emph{exact} (full resolution, then averaged, as
  E5's truth), \emph{precomputed} (E5's stored slope pyramid). Per-stage timings (read, decode, kernel, colour,
  encode) for every tile.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Gates and results.} 24 new tests (143 in total). Every auto-mode tile equals the existing engine's result on the
whole level, cell for cell, for all 8 operator configurations, on every level of a test pyramid with data gaps and
partial edge tiles. \textbf{Parallel gate} (\code{bench/check\_tile\_parallel}, Chamoli 2\,m, 64 tiles, warm blocks): 8
threads \textbf{5.4$\times$} faster than 1 (8.2\,ms $\to$ 1.5\,ms per tile of throughput; gate $\geq$5$\times$); with
NumPy quantising and Pillow encoding it was 4.3$\times$. \textbf{Block cache:} 64 neighbouring tiles decode \textbf{100
blocks instead of up to 576}; warm tiles decode none.

\subsection{Scheduler and server (final plan Phase 2)}\label{sec:p5server}
Files: \code{demlab/sched.py}, \code{service.py}, \code{cpus.py}, \code{server/app.py} (\code{python -m server}). Design
and why:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two pools pinned to core classes.} Foreground (visible) jobs go to 8 workers pinned to the 12
  performance-core threads (CPUs 0, 1, 10--19); background (prefetch) jobs to 3 workers on the 8 efficiency cores (CPUs
  2--9). They never share workers, because E3 showed that efficiency cores mixed in slow the foreground. Pinning is per
  thread (\code{SetThreadAffinityMask}); the low-power cores (20--21) are not used.
  \item \textbf{Queue order:} newest generation first, then distance from the view centre / pan direction.
  \item \textbf{Single-flight with tickets.} Each request holds a ticket on a job; identical requests share the job. A
  foreground request for a tile queued as prefetch \emph{promotes} it. A job is cancelled only when its last live ticket
  is gone.
  \item \textbf{Generations.} Every zoom commit raises the generation of the viewer's session, announced with
  \code{POST /session/\{sid\}/gen}; older work of that session is superseded (HTTP 409).
  \item \textbf{Cancel tokens}, checked before dequeue, after each block read, before the kernel and before encoding.
  GDAL reads cannot be interrupted, so the worst case is one block decode. Exact-mode tiles are computed in strips one
  stored block row deep and stop at the next strip.
  \item \textbf{Prefetch yields:} a background worker does not start a new tile while any foreground tile is queued or
  running (a running one is not interrupted).
  \item \textbf{Backpressure and memory guard.} The prefetch queue holds at most 256 jobs (oldest generation, then
  farthest, dropped first). Prefetch pauses below 1.0\,GB free RAM, and paused prefetch requests get HTTP 503 at once.
  \item \textbf{Event-driven disconnect detection:} a closed connection (a browser abort) cancels the job (HTTP 499
  internally).
  \item \textbf{API:} \code{GET /info} (datasets, levels, expected E5 error, legends), \code{GET /overview} (coarsest level,
  the base layer), \code{GET /tiles/\{dataset\}/\{level\}/\{tx\}/\{ty\}.png} (ETag, cache headers, \code{Server-Timing},
  \code{X-Expected-Error}), \code{POST /prefetch}, \code{POST /view} (a box as PNG or GeoTIFF, rendered from the shared
  block cache), \code{GET /stats}, and the viewer itself at \code{/viewer/}. Settings by environment variables
  (cache budgets, worker counts, guard, yielding).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Gates and results} (17 new tests, 160 in total; \code{bench/check\_server}, real server, Chamoli 2\,m):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Isolation:} foreground p95 16.6\,ms idle vs 16.6\,ms with 256 prefetch tiles queued (\textbf{+0.4\%};
  median of 3 rounds $\times$ 100 tiles; gate $\leq$+10\%). Before yielding it swung between +6\% and +15\%.
  \item \textbf{Cancellation:} an aborted request still queued is dropped in a median \textbf{37\,ms} (gate 50\,ms). A
  running exact-mode tile stops at its next strip (7--155\,ms).
  \item \textbf{Core classes} detected per thread as above; \textbf{memory guard} works (with 1.9\,GB free it paused
  prefetch as designed).
  \item \textbf{Deviation} from the spec's URL: tiles carry the dataset name, because two datasets are served.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Web viewer (final plan Phase 3)}\label{sec:p5viewer}
\code{viz/web/} (plain ES modules, no build step), served by the tile server: \code{python -m server}, then open
\url{http://127.0.0.1:8765/viewer/}. Design and why:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Camera:} UTM centre and metres per device pixel; device-pixel ratio capped at 1 for level choice
  (HiDPI screens would multiply the work).
  \item \textbf{Tile layer:} every frame draws the needed level's tiles; a missing tile falls back to a coarser cached
  level, then a finer cached level, then the overview (always loaded), so no cell is ever blank. During gestures the
  preview is the browser's scaled \code{drawImage} of the fallback tiles.
  \item \textbf{Bitmap cache:} byte-accounted LRU of 256\,MiB, other levels evicted first, \code{close()} on evict.
  \item \textbf{Gestures:} wheel (cursor-anchored), pinch, drag, keyboard. \textbf{Zoom commits once after a trailing
  debounce} (150\,ms after E6); \textbf{pan requests new tiles at once} (no debounce: a pan does not change the
  level). Foreground tiles are requested from the centre out; every request carries the session's generation and is
  aborted (\code{AbortController}) when no longer visible.
  \item \textbf{Prefetch ring:} after a commit, the 8 view-sized rectangles around the view at the committed level,
  velocity-first, at most 2 in flight (leaving 4 of the browser's 6 connections for the foreground), recentred at a
  25\% offset (at most once per 100\,ms), shrunk to the 4 cardinals above 150 tiles. Off in exact mode, in a hidden tab,
  and for 2\,s after a slow foreground (p95 of the last 20 tiles $>$250\,ms) or a 503. Requests time out after 10\,s.
  \item \textbf{UI:} operator, method, mode (auto / exact / precomputed), legend, level, cell size and expected error,
  breadcrumb, Back, Whole map, and Shift + drag box zoom.
  \item \textbf{One policy file} (\code{viz/web/policy.json}) read by the browser and by the E6 virtual client.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Checks.} 6 Node unit tests (level choice equals the server's on a fixture; lattice, camera, cache eviction,
prefetch ring). In-browser self-test (\code{/viewer/?selftest=1}) in headless Chrome against the real server
(Chamoli 2\,m, hillshade), all four pass:

\subsection{QGIS plugin (final plan Phase 4)}\label{sec:p5qgis}
\code{qgis\_plugin/geoguessers\_view} (``GeoGuessers terrain''; install with \code{qgis\_plugin\textbackslash install.ps1},

2\,m; HTTP 400 with the reason), so one request cannot build a multi-GB mosaic.

full-resolution DEM (1.22\,M cells, max difference 0); a box drawn on a WGS\,84 map gets exactly the level the rule
gives; both datasets work; an oversized box and a box outside the data give messages instead of layers. Still to do by
hand: one look in the QGIS GUI.

\section{The whole story (what to say in the presentation)}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Problem:} a 1\,m DEM of our area is 43\,GB, 2.6$\times$ the laptop's RAM.
  \item \textbf{Standard tools} (gdaldem, QGIS) don't crash: they stream. But slope takes 3.5\,min on one thread, and
  displaying without a pyramid takes 32\,s per redraw. Bottleneck: single-threaded computation; for display, reading.
  \item \textbf{Our engine} uses all cores (and optionally the GPU) in a bounded pipeline: 3.5$\times$ (CPU) to 4.75$\times$
  (GPU) faster, identical results, constant memory. The naive ``load it all'' approach fails beyond $\sim$0.6\,B cells.
  \item \textbf{But} the bottleneck moves to data movement: decompression, PCIe copies, writing 43\,GB of output. A GPU only
  pays when computation is a large share (fused products) or windows are large enough ($\geq512^2$).
  \item \textbf{So} don't process everything: read and compute only what is viewed, from the right pyramid level. That
  makes every view cost 5--8\,ms regardless of zoom, with a known, measured accuracy cost (slope too low by
  $\sim$2.5$^\circ$ + 1.5$^\circ$/level), and no storage for precomputed products.
  \item \textbf{The viewer} turns this into a map: continuous pan and zoom over a fixed $512^2$ tile lattice, every tile
  computed on demand. A 150\,ms trailing debounce cuts the server's work per zoom gesture 3.3$\times$; a velocity-first
  ring of neighbouring views makes panning seamless (98--100\% of new tiles already in the browser); prefetch runs
  on the efficiency cores and never while the user waits, so it does not slow the visible tiles.
  \item \textbf{And the bottleneck is still data movement:} the viewer's remaining latency is decoding compressed
  blocks and waiting for one of the browser's 6 connections, not computing slope or hillshade. The same lesson at
  the scale of one tile.
\end{enumerate}

waited for each other. A \code{nogil} Numba quantiser and our own PNG writer with \code{zlib} (Z\_RLE: 2--3$\times$
faster, 5--15\% larger tiles): 5.4$\times$. Check every stage of a threaded path for the GIL, not just the kernel. \\
Windows gives E- and LP-cores the same efficiency class & A split by class alone would mix the very slow LP-cores into the
prefetch pool. We tell them apart by their separate last-level cache (CPUs 20--21) and leave them unused. \\
Cancelled job still running could be joined & A new request for the same tile could attach to a job that was cancelled
but still finishing its stage, and that job's clean-up could remove the newer job from the
table. Now a request never joins a cancelled job (a new one is created), and clean-up removes the entry only if it is
still the same job. \\
Isolation gate noisy (+6 to +15\%) & Even on separate cores, background threads share the GIL and memory bandwidth with
the foreground. Prefetch now yields (no new prefetch tile while foreground work is pending): +0.4\%. \\
Exact-mode tiles slow to cancel & A running tile can stop only between stages; exact tiles are now computed in strips
one stored block row deep, so they stop at the next strip (7--155\,ms). \\
Disconnects found by polling every 20\,ms & Gave 58--80\,ms (gate 50\,ms). Event-driven disconnect detection: median
37\,ms. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 5: web viewer and QGIS plugin (final plan Phases 3--4)}} \\
Memory guard at 2.5\,GB kept prefetch paused & This laptop often runs with $<$2\,GB free. Default lowered to 1.0\,GB;
paused prefetch now gets 503 at once, so the client backs off for 2\,s instead of holding two connections open;
prefetch requests time out after 10\,s. \\
Prefetch never resumed in some cases & If prefetch could not start right after a commit (backing off, hidden tab), it did
not start. Now it is marked pending and starts on a later frame. \\
Box zoom produced two commits & The animation's moves also started the debounce timer. Animated moves (box zoom, Back,
Whole map) now suppress the timer and commit once at the end. \\
Blank map at start & The first view was fitted before the canvas size was known. The viewer now reads the canvas size
before fitting the first view (and follows resizes). \\
Headless Chrome in virtual-time mode & It distorts every network wait. Switched to real-time headless Chrome driven over
the DevTools protocol with the \code{websockets} package the server already uses: Playwright (which would have needed the
team's OK to install) was not needed. \\
One QGIS box could build a multi-GB mosaic & \code{POST /view} refuses boxes over 64\,M cells (HTTP 400 with the
reason). \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{\textit{Phase 5: E6 (final plan Phase 5)}} \\
Virtual client's timers up to 16\,ms early & Python 3.12's Windows event-loop clock moves in 15.6\,ms steps and fires
timers up to one step early, so the debounce fired early. First sweep stopped after 17 runs (kept in \code{old
files/results\_superseded/}) and rerun on our own high-resolution loop ($\sim$1\,ms). \\
Back-off threshold of 100\,ms & Sat at the cold-start p95 ($\sim$100\,ms: a fresh view's blocks decoding while 15--20 tiles
queue on 6 connections), so prefetch switched off at random (pilot) and the fast reversing pan fell to a 0.50 hit rate.
Raised to 250\,ms before the sweep (the sweep still measures 100\,ms as a variant). \\
The spec's gate 1 compares different tiles & Per-trace foreground p95 with prefetch on vs off: with prefetch on, pans
need almost no foreground tiles, so the two sides are different tile sets. Used the like-for-like box trace (identical
tiles). \\
Steady 60\,Hz ticks hide the debounce choice & Gaps of 17\,ms make 100--250\,ms behave the same. Notched-wheel traces
(30--150 and 120--300\,ms gaps, 72 runs) added after the main sweep; they set 150\,ms. \\
Laptop has only $\sim$2\,GB free with the editor open & E6 sets the prefetch memory guard to 0.5\,GB; no prefetch request
was refused in any run (free memory never below 633\,MB). \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\section{Validity and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One laptop.} All timings are for this machine (Core Ultra 9 185H, 16\,GB, NVMe SSD, RTX 4060 Laptop,
  Windows 11). Ratios (speed-ups, stage shares) transfer better than absolute times.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic 2\,m/1\,m} grids add cells but no detail: timing only. Accuracy (Phase 4) uses real data only.
  \item \textbf{One region of steep terrain} (mean true slope 38$^\circ$ on Chamoli): flatter regions would show smaller
  pyramid errors; the per-class tables carry over.
  \item \textbf{Single warm runs} at 1\,m (protocol choice, stated). Repeats agree closely (median IQR 1.2--1.6\%).
  \item \textbf{Only GDAL/QGIS} as the baseline: other tools (WhiteboxTools, GRASS, xarray-spatial) were not benchmarked.
  \item \textbf{Truth is itself a DEM} with noise; the 8\,m DEM's truth is only 8\,m.
  \item \textbf{Python start-up} ($\sim$0.5\,s) is included in every engine time, like gdaldem's start-up: fair, but it
  dominates the small grids.
  \item \textbf{E6: one client on one laptop.} The result cache and the spare foreground workers are there for several
  clients (browser + QGIS); E6 runs one.
  \item \textbf{E6: short synthetic traces} (1--11\,s), seeded rather than recorded from people. Caches never fill, so
  long sessions (evictions, the result cache) are not measured.
  \item \textbf{E6: simulated client.} The virtual client is a port of the viewer; the headless-Chrome check agrees on all
  counts and within 10\% on view-complete times, but covers three traces.
  \item \textbf{E6: cold start only.} Every run starts with an empty server; a returning user would see faster tiles.
  \item \textbf{E6: CPU only.} The GPU path is not used for tiles: at $512\times512$ per tile, E4 predicts little gain once the
  transfer is counted (1.3$\times$ including the copies).
  \item \textbf{Not done:} the QGIS box-drawing baseline listed in the plan (QGIS's own rendering is covered by E1q), and
  one look at the plugin in the QGIS GUI by hand.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Likely questions and answers}
\begin{longtable}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{10.2cm}}
\toprule
Question & Answer \\
\midrule
\endhead
Why not just give gdaldem more threads? & We tested \code{GDAL\_NUM\_THREADS=ALL\_CPUS} and a 4\,GB cache: 2--4\% \emph{slower}. Only
decompression is parallel there; gdaldem's computation is single-threaded by design. \\
Is the comparison fair? & Same machine, grids, harness and cores (12 P-threads); gdaldem at its fastest settings; engine times
include Python start-up; results identical on every cell. \\
Why use upsampled 1\,m data? & No free 1\,m DEM exists for the area. Run time depends on cell count, which we verified on real vs
upsampled data; accuracy uses real data only. \\
Why is the GPU only 1.4$\times$ faster? & The kernel is 50$\times$ faster, but 95\% of the GPU's time is copying over PCIe and the
run is limited by decompressing and writing. Amdahl's law. \\
When does the GPU make sense? & Windows $\geq512^2$, several products at once, heavy operators, or results that stay in memory
(display), not written to disk. \\
Why does coarse-level slope come out lower? & Averaging smooths the terrain before the gradient is taken; steep, rough slopes lose
the most. \\
Is that error acceptable? & For display, yes (the map keeps every landform; error is shown). For thresholds (e.g.\ $>$45$^\circ$),
no: use the exact mode or a precomputed slope pyramid. \\
Why not precompute everything? & Exact and slightly faster, but a full pass and $\sim$1\,GB per derivative and parameter; on demand
needs no storage and supports any parameter. \\
Why did you drop box zoom as the main interaction? & The professor asked for map-like continuous pan and zoom. The fixed
$512^2$ lattice keeps the cost per tile at the same few ms at every zoom (E5), so continuous viewing is possible. Box zoom
remains as Shift + drag and as \code{POST /view} for QGIS. \\
Why debounce the zoom? & A wheel gesture sends many ticks; committing on each one starts and aborts a new view every
17\,ms. The server then renders 62 foreground tiles per gesture instead of 19 (3.3$\times$ more), most of them for views the
user never stops at. During the gesture the screen shows the tiles it has, rescaled, so nothing goes blank. \\
Why 150\,ms and not 100 or 200? & With 60\,Hz bursts 100--250\,ms all give 19 tiles. A notched wheel (ticks 30--150\,ms
apart) separates them: 100\,ms commits between notches (36 tiles, as bad as none); 150\,ms and longer coalesce the
gesture (15). 200\,ms adds 40--50\,ms of delay for no fewer tiles. \\
Why doesn't prefetch slow the foreground? & Three reasons: it runs on the efficiency cores while the foreground runs on the
performance cores; a prefetch worker does not start a tile while any foreground tile is pending; and the client stops
prefetch at the first input of a gesture and restarts it only when the view is complete (only 3\% of foreground tiles
ever overlap a prefetch request). On identical tiles server p95 is 90\,ms with prefetch vs 95\,ms without: it even helps,
because it has already decoded shared blocks. Not yielding costs 9\%. \\
Isn't prefetch wasteful? & Yes, 2--5 tiles fetched per tile used (precision 0.20--0.56), because a ring of 8 neighbours
loads directions the user does not take. The cost is about 3 E-core CPU-seconds per 5\,s pan; the gain is 98--100\%
of new tiles ready while panning instead of 0--50\%. Velocity-first ordering keeps it useful at speed (0.99 vs 0.91). \\
Why do the 6 connections matter? & A browser opens at most 6 HTTP/1.1 connections to one server; other tile requests wait.
That wait is why client p95 is about 40\,ms above server p95, and why 4, 6 or 8 server workers give the same client
latency. Prefetch takes at most 2 connections, leaving 4 for visible tiles. We keep 8 workers so a second client (QGIS)
does not queue. \\
Why not the GPU for the tiles? & A $512^2$ tile sits at the CPU/GPU crossover from E4 (GPU only 1.3$\times$ faster
including the copies), and a tile's cost is mostly decoding compressed blocks, not the kernel. In E5 the 12-thread kernel
took $\sim$0.5\,ms of a 5--8\,ms tile; the server instead runs one single-threaded kernel per tile (median 2.3\,ms,
p95 6.2\,ms for foreground tiles in E6) on 8 tiles at once, which gives more throughput than threading inside one
tile. E6 is CPU only; a GPU
tile path is a stretch item. \\
How does the QGIS plugin work? & A panel and a box tool. The box is reprojected to UTM 44N and sent with the map's width to
the same server's \code{POST /view} in a background \code{QgsTask}; the server picks the level that matches the box on
screen (or full resolution) and returns a GeoTIFF that is added as a styled layer with level, cell size and expected E5
error in its metadata. A full-resolution box is identical to gdaldem's slope (max difference 0). \\
What happens with little free memory? & All caches are byte-budgeted (blocks 1024\,MiB, tiles 512\,MiB, browser 256\,MiB;
server peak in E6 982\,MiB). Below 1.0\,GB free RAM the server pauses prefetch and answers prefetch requests with 503 at
once; the client backs off for 2\,s. Visible tiles are always served. \code{POST /view} refuses boxes over 64\,M cells. \\
How do you know the virtual client behaves like a browser? & It is a port of the viewer's code reading the same
\code{policy.json}, with 6 connections and real aborts. We replayed three traces in real headless Chrome over the DevTools
protocol: same commits, same foreground requests (15, 0, 35), same prefetched tiles, view complete within 10\%, first
frames 18--25\,ms sooner in Chrome. So E6 latencies are slight upper bounds. \\
What happens when the user zooms away while tiles are computing? & The zoom commit raises the generation; the server
drops older queued work of that session, the browser aborts requests no longer visible (the server sees the disconnect
and cancels in a median 37\,ms), and running tiles stop at their next stage check. \\
How do you know your results are correct? & 119 tests; every cell of four full grids compared with gdaldem; differences explained
by gdaldem's float32 rounding and reproduced exactly. For the viewer: 161 tests in total; every auto tile equals the
engine's result cell for cell; a QGIS full-resolution box equals gdaldem's slope exactly. \\
How repeatable are the timings? & Median IQR 1.2--1.6\% of the median; cold cache verified; background interference detected and
removed; disturbed attempts kept as evidence. \\
What would change on another laptop? & Absolute times; the shape of the results (where the bottleneck is) should hold on similar
hardware; with a slow HDD, reading would matter more. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
