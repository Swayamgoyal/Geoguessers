\section{The project in one page}
\paragraph{The question.} Our professor asked us to work like a systems team: take a very large, high-resolution
DEM (Himalaya, 30\,m down to 1\,m), use only a laptop, and find out (a) where today's standard tools (GDAL, QGIS)
stop being practical and \emph{why} (what is the real bottleneck: disk, RAM, CPU, GPU transfer, rendering?), and (b)
how to process and display only what the user is looking at, using a \textbf{pyramid} (the same DEM stored at several
resolutions), computing slope/aspect/hillshade \textbf{on demand} and using the CPU and GPU where each actually helps.

\paragraph{What is finished.} Phase 0 (software setup), Phase 1 (data) and Phase 2 (baseline benchmarks of the
standard tools). Phase 2 answers part (a). Parts of (b) start in Phase 3.

\paragraph{The answer so far, in three lines.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \code{gdaldem} (GDAL's terrain tool, which QGIS also calls) \textbf{never runs out of memory}: it handles
  10.8 billion cells (43\,GB) with 0.83\,GB of RAM. It just gets slower: 3.5\,min for one slope pass at 1\,m.
  \item Its bottleneck is \textbf{computation on a single CPU thread} (73\% of the time; it uses 1 of our 22
  threads). The disk and RAM are nowhere near their limits.
  \item For \textbf{display}, the bottleneck is \textbf{reading full-resolution data}: without a pyramid QGIS needs
  32\,s to draw the whole 1\,m area; with a pyramid, 0.09\,s at any size.
\end{enumerate}
So the plan for the rest of the project is clear: build our own engine that uses all cores (and the GPU), reads and
decompresses in parallel, computes several products in one pass, and works from the pyramid on demand.

Halo & When a big grid is cut into tiles, each tile needs one extra ring of cells from its neighbours so 3$\times$3

can then come from RAM (``warm'') instead of disk (``cold''). \\

Peak working set & The most RAM a process held at any moment; our ``peak memory''. \\

\subsection{The terrain operators and their formulas}\label{sec:formulas}
Label the 3$\times$3 window around cell $e$ as
\[
\begin{array}{ccc} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i \end{array}
\qquad \text{cell size } \Delta x = \Delta y .
\]
\textbf{Horn (1981):}
\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{(c+2f+i)-(a+2d+g)}{8\Delta x},\qquad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{(g+2h+i)-(a+2b+c)}{8\Delta y}. \]
\textbf{Zevenbergen--Thorne (1987):}
\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{f-d}{2\Delta x},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{h-b}{2\Delta y}. \]

Then \textbf{slope} $= \arctan\sqrt{(\partial z/\partial x)^2 + (\partial z/\partial y)^2}$; \textbf{aspect} is the
direction of the steepest descent, from $\operatorname{atan2}$ of the two gradients; \textbf{hillshade} (light from
azimuth $\phi$, altitude $\theta$) $= 255\,[\sin\theta\cos s + \cos\theta\sin s\cos(\phi - \text{aspect})]$;
\textbf{TRI} (Riley) $= \sqrt{\sum (z_n - z_e)^2}$ and (Wilson) the mean of $|z_n - z_e|$ over the 8 neighbours $n$;
\textbf{TPI} $= z_e - \text{mean}(z_n)$; \textbf{roughness} $= \max - \min$ of the window.

Two consequences used later: (1) every operator divides by the cell size, so slope computed on a coarser pyramid
level is \emph{different} (smoother), not just faster; that is exactly what experiment E5 will measure. (2) All six
need the same few neighbour differences, so computing them together (one pass) shares most of the work.

\section{What we did, in order}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Setup.} Python environment \code{geo} (GDAL 3.13.3, rasterio, Numba, CuPy, FastAPI) via Miniforge at
\code{D:\textbackslash miniforge3} (the default path failed because our user folder name has a space), QGIS 3.44.14.
Checked: CuPy runs on the RTX 4060; QGIS slope matched an OpenTopography reference to $10^{-5}$ degrees.

\item \textbf{Choosing data.} The first downloads (an Everest 90\,m crop, two single 8\,m strips) were too small or
patchy. We switched to the NASA High Mountain Asia 8\,m \emph{mosaic} tile-571 (Uttarakhand), downloaded
Copernicus 30\,m and 90\,m for exactly the same box through the OpenTopography API, and found a free real 2\,m DEM of
the Chamoli valley inside the tile (Bhushan \& Shean, Zenodo).

\item \textbf{One area, many resolutions.} Run time depends on the number of cells, not on which mountain it is.
Using one area means grid size is the only thing that changes between measurements, and the real 2\,m data gives
us a ``truth'' for accuracy later.

\item \textbf{Checking the data.} Tile-571 is 90.4\% valid (gaps in the south-east corner). The 8\,m tile is 24.5\,m
below Copernicus (datum difference, see glossary). The Chamoli 2\,m averaged to 8\,m matches the 8\,m DEM to a median
of $-0.7$\,m (NMAD 5.1\,m): the two are consistent. Chamoli was re-gridded with nearest-neighbour (only 0.09/0.16\,m
off our grid) so its heights are untouched.

\item \textbf{Are synthetic grids OK?} No free 1\,m DEM exists here. We tested on a 16\,M-cell crop: an 8\,m grid
rebuilt from 16\,m takes the same gdaldem time as the real one, but its slopes are off by 2.3--3.0$^\circ$ RMSE, and
nearest-neighbour data compresses 26\% better (fake fast reads). Decision: synthetic 2\,m and 1\,m grids, made with
\emph{cubic} resampling, are used for \emph{timing only}.

\item \textbf{Building the harness} (Sec.~\ref{sec:harness}): exact per-process measurements, cold-cache flushing,
resumable sweeps.

\item \textbf{First E1 pass} (74 min, 560 runs, 0 failures). Then a critical review found weak spots: the ``read''
reference was wrong, one run was an unexplained 2$\times$ outlier, conditions were not recorded.

\item \textbf{Fixing the method.} Core test (Sec.~\ref{sec:pinning}) $\to$ pin to performance cores. New pure read
reference. Defender's CPU use measured (tiny during runs). Run conditions saved with every sweep.

\item \textbf{Second attempt (E1 + E2 + QGIS)} ran into a full disk: 108\,GB of deleted outputs were held in
\code{\$Deleted} while Defender scanned them. We stopped, deleted those results, freed space, added a safeguard
(wait until deleted space is really released before the next run), and re-ran.

\item \textbf{Chamoli anomaly.} Some Chamoli runs were 25--35\% slower in streaks, with 1--2\,GB of disk reads that
were not gdaldem's: background scanning of previous outputs. With the safeguard the re-run was clean, but E2 (which
writes 10.8\,GB outputs) was then stopped by the safeguard itself (35.6\,GB held for $>$15 min).

\item \textbf{Final setup and full clean re-run.} We excluded only the two scratch folders from Defender, restarted
the laptop (frees \code{\$Deleted}) and re-ran everything under identical conditions: \textbf{832 runs, 0 failures}.
A quality check confirmed the data are clean (Sec.~\ref{sec:quality}). The disturbed attempts are kept as evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{How the measurements work}\label{sec:harness}
\subsection{One run}
\code{bench/harness.py} starts the tool (e.g.\ \code{gdaldem slope in.tif out.tif}) as its own process, pinned to the
performance cores, and waits. When it ends, the harness reads from Windows, for that process only: wall time, peak
memory, total CPU time (so CPU time / wall time = how many cores it used on average), bytes it read and wrote. It also
counts bytes physically read from the disks during the run. If the disk read much more than the process did, someone
else was using the disk: that is our ``foreign disk read'' interference signal. One row per run goes into
\code{results/runs.csv}.

An early version sampled these counters every 50\,ms and missed the end of short runs; reading the exact totals from the
finished process fixed that.

\subsection{Cold cache}
To measure a true first read we must empty the Windows file cache. That needs admin rights, so we registered a
scheduled task (\code{\textbackslash GeoGuessers\textbackslash GeoGuessers-FlushCache}) that runs Sysinternals
\code{RAMMap -Et} (empty standby list) with admin rights; the benchmark triggers it without being admin. We verified it:
after a flush the 422\,MB 8\,m file was read entirely from disk again (at $\sim$2\,GB/s). (An early mistake used
\code{-Ec}, which is not a RAMMap option; RAMMap then just waited.)

\subsection{Protocol}
Real grids: 5 cold runs (cache emptied before each), then 1 warm-up and 5 warm runs. Synthetic 2\,m/1\,m: 3 cold + 1
warm (the last cold run doubles as warm-up). This trimmed protocol saved $\sim$5 hours; operator comparisons do not
depend on size, so they come from the real grids. Medians are reported.

\subsection{Why we pin to performance cores}\label{sec:pinning}
We ran the same 8\,m slope job pinned to each of the 22 logical CPUs. Performance cores: 3.7--4.1\,s. Efficiency cores:
$\sim$6.6\,s (1.7$\times$). Low-power cores: up to 10.5\,s (2.7$\times$). Unpinned, Windows may move a hidden background
process onto slow cores mid-run: that explains one first-pass run of 108.9\,s instead of $\sim$56\,s. Pinning makes
every tool run at its best, so any speedup we claim later for our engine is \emph{conservative}.

\subsection{Why only two folders are excluded from Defender}
Defender's CPU use during a run is tiny (0.02--0.05 of a core). The problem is \emph{after} the run: it scans each new
10--40\,GB output, and if we delete the file meanwhile, its space stays reserved until the scan ends. That filled our
disk once (108\,GB) and later slowed the next runs (scan competing for disk and turbo budget). The files in
\code{bench\_out} and \code{e2} are written by our own GDAL tools and deleted within minutes, so excluding just those two
folders loses no protection. The harness additionally waits until deleted space is released before starting the next
run, and stops cleanly (instead of filling the disk) if that takes more than 15 minutes.

\subsection{Splitting a run into stages}
gdaldem cannot tell us its internal timings, so we use two reference runs per grid:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{read}: a small script that reads and decompresses every full-resolution block, nothing else
  (\code{bench/read\_blocks.py}); it reports its own loop time, excluding Python start-up;
  \item \textbf{copy}: \code{gdal\_translate} to an uncompressed GeoTIFF: the same read plus a same-size write.
\end{itemize}
Then \emph{write + tool overhead} $\approx$ copy $-$ read and \emph{compute} $\approx$ gdaldem slope $-$ copy. The first
read reference (\code{gdalinfo -checksum}) spent over half its time computing the checksum, which made ``write'' come out
negative; that is why it was replaced.

\subsection{Experiments}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{E1} (560 runs): 10 gdaldem configurations (slope, aspect, hillshade $\times$ Horn/ZT; TRI Riley/Wilson;
  TPI; roughness) on 6 grids, cold and warm, plus read/copy references. At 1\,m only slope and hillshade (Horn).
  \item \textbf{E2} (224 runs): 7 storage layouts of the 8\,m and 2\,m grids; for each, the time to write it, a pure read,
  and gdaldem slope. Each 2\,m copy ($\le$10\,GB) was moved to the external drive right after its runs.
  \item \textbf{E1-QGIS} (48 runs): QGIS (headless) opens the layer and draws one 1920$\times$1080 image, either the whole
  area or a 1920\,m$\times$1080\,m zoom, with the pyramid or with it hidden (GDAL option \code{OVERVIEW\_LEVEL=NONE}).
  GDAL's statistics caches are disabled so every open starts clean.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results, and what each one means}
\subsection{E1: how gdaldem scales}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_scaling.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_memory.png}
\end{figure}
\textbf{How to read the left chart.} Both axes are logarithmic. A straight line at 45$^\circ$ means ``time
proportional to cells''. Slope and hillshade lines are straight from 12\,M cells up: \textbf{linear scaling}, about
20--25\,ns per cell. At the far left (90\,m) the line bends because a run lasts only 0.07\,s and most of that is starting
the program. Numbers: slope 4.0\,s (8\,m), 6.6\,s (2\,m real), 54\,s (2\,m synthetic), 208\,s (1\,m).

\textbf{How to read the right chart.} The dotted line is the size of the grid itself. gdaldem's memory (blue)
\emph{stops growing} at $\sim$830\,MB while the grid keeps growing past the 16\,GB line. Why: gdaldem reads a few rows,
computes, writes, and moves on (streaming), keeping only GDAL's block cache, whose default size is 5\% of RAM
($\approx$ 0.8\,GB). \textbf{Meaning:} on this laptop gdaldem cannot ``break'' on memory at any size. Our question
``where does it break?'' therefore has the answer ``it doesn't; it becomes slow'', and the memory-hungry approaches
(loading whole arrays, which we will test in Phase 3) are where real breaking points are.

\textbf{Cold vs warm.} They differ by 2\% or less. Two reasons: the NVMe SSD reads at $\sim$2\,GB/s, so reading is a
small part of the run anyway; and at 2\,m/1\,m the file is bigger than what Windows keeps cached, so even ``warm'' runs
read 3.6/13.6\,GB from disk. Our pilot (lit review) saw 3$\times$; that was timed through QGIS on another laptop, so its
first runs most likely included loading the programs.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_stages.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{e1_operators.png}
\end{figure}
\textbf{Stage split (left).} At every real size, $\sim$15\% is reading + decompressing, 11--14\% writing (and tool
overhead), and \textbf{71--74\% computing}. This contradicts the common claim that data movement dominates; for gdaldem
on a fast SSD, it is the arithmetic on one thread. \textbf{Meaning:} parallelising the computation is the biggest
lever, but read and write are $\sim$27\% together, so if only the computation were parallel, the best possible speedup
would be about $1/0.27 \approx 3.7\times$ (Amdahl's law). To go further we must also parallelise decompression and
writing, or avoid writing at all (on-demand display).

\textbf{Operators (right).} All ten cost 17.5--27\,ns per cell. Horn vs ZT differ by at most 6\% (ZT is slightly
cheaper because it uses 4 neighbours instead of 8). Slope and aspect cost more than hillshade, TRI, TPI and roughness,
partly because they write 4-byte floats while hillshade writes 1-byte values. \textbf{Meaning:} the formula is not
where speed comes from; implementation and data movement are.

\textbf{Pinning.} On the 2\,m synthetic grid, unpinned cold runs ranged 55.8--108.9\,s (74\% spread); pinned,
53.7--55.2\,s (2.8\%). Proof that core placement, not the tool, caused the outlier.

\subsection{E2: does the file layout matter?}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{e2_storage.png}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Size:} uncompressed 10.3--10.4\,GB; LZW 4.8\,GB; DEFLATE 3.7\,GB; ZSTD 3.6\,GB (2.9$\times$ smaller).
  \item \textbf{Read:} uncompressed 3.3--4.0\,s (pure disk at $\sim$2.5\,GB/s); ZSTD 7.8\,s; DEFLATE 11.4\,s; LZW 20.1\,s.
  Decompression costs CPU, and LZW decodes slowest.
  \item \textbf{gdaldem slope:} uncompressed strips 47\,s (fastest), ZSTD 54\,s, DEFLATE 57\,s, LZW 66\,s (+39\%).
  gdaldem decompresses inside its single thread, so slower decoding directly adds to its time.
  \item \textbf{Write:} compressed files take $\sim$9$\times$ longer to create (ZSTD 124\,s vs 14\,s). That is a one-time
  cost when the pyramid is built.
  \item \textbf{Tiles vs strips:} for gdaldem's row-by-row pass, strips are fine (even slightly faster). Tiles matter for
  reading small windows, which is what the on-demand viewer does; 512 is slightly better than 256.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Meaning:} layout changes gdaldem time by up to $\sim$40\%, less than computation but not negligible. Tiled ZSTD,
which we already use for the prepared grids, is the right compressed format: smallest and fastest to decode. Avoid LZW.
In our parallel engine, decompression can be spread across cores, which should shrink this cost.

\subsection{QGIS: why the pyramid matters}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{e1q_render.png}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Whole area, with pyramid:} 0.09\,s at 8\,m, 2\,m and 1\,m, peak 200\,MB. QGIS picks the pyramid level
  whose cells match the screen pixels and reads only that small level.
  \item \textbf{Whole area, no pyramid:} 0.79\,s, 8.8\,s, \textbf{31.9\,s}; peak $\sim$980\,MB. To draw 1920 pixels across
  105{,}120 columns it must still decompress every tile of the full grid; 31.9\,s is close to the 29\,s a pure read of the
  1\,m file takes.
  \item \textbf{Zoomed in:} $\sim$0.1\,s with or without pyramid, because only the window on screen is read.
  \item \textbf{Opening a layer:} 0.02--0.03\,s always (QGIS does not read the data when opening).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Meaning:} the display bottleneck is ``how much full-resolution data must be read for this view''. A pyramid makes
zoomed-out views cost the same at any grid size, which is exactly the meeting's proposed design. Since slope for a
256$\times$256 tile costs only $\sim$1.6\,ms (65{,}536 cells $\times$ 25\,ns), computing terrain products on the fly per
tile is cheap; reading the right window at the right level is what matters.

\subsection{Is the data trustworthy?}\label{sec:quality}
\code{python -m bench.analysis.quality\_check} computes, per grid, the spread between repeats and the number of runs with
foreign disk reads. Final run: median spread 0.2--3.3\% for every grid $\ge$169\,M cells (Chamoli improved from 7.8\% in
the disturbed attempt to 2.5\%). Remaining outliers are single slow runs (one 8\,m aspect run with background disk
activity; one Chamoli TRI run) or the very first cold start of Python/QGIS, which only affects the total time, not the
tool's own time we report. Medians absorb these. Runs under $\sim$10\,M cells measure mostly program start-up and are not
used for per-cell claims.

\section{Future scope, and how we will do it}
\subsection{Phase 3: our own engine (E3 CPU, E4 GPU)}
\textbf{Goal:} beat gdaldem on the same machine by attacking the measured bottleneck.

\textbf{Design} (package \code{demlab/}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{io.py}: read the COG window by window (e.g.\ 2048$\times$2048 cells) with a 1-cell halo, aligned to the
  512-cell tiles; reading/decompressing several windows at once in a thread pool (GDAL releases Python's lock while
  decoding, so threads really run in parallel).
  \item \code{kernels/numpy\_k.py}: the reference implementation, vectorised with array slicing: the Horn formula is
  applied to shifted views of the whole window at once. Simple, easy to verify, single-threaded, whole window in memory.
  \item \code{kernels/numba\_k.py}: the same formulas in a loop compiled with \code{@njit(parallel=True)}, with
  \code{prange} over rows so all cores share the work. Computes slope, aspect and hillshade \emph{together} from one
  gradient pass (fused kernel).
  \item \code{kernels/cuda\_k.py}: CuPy element-wise / raw kernels on the GPU; copy window to GPU, compute, copy back.
  \item \code{tiling.py}: loops over windows, runs the kernel, writes results (tiled, optionally compressed in parallel)
  or returns them in memory for the viewer.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Correctness first:} on the 8\,m and Chamoli grids our outputs must equal gdaldem's within float tolerance
(e.g.\ $\le 10^{-3}$ degrees), including nodata handling and edges (gdaldem leaves the outer ring empty unless asked).
Tests in \code{tests/}.

\textbf{What we will measure:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item E3 (CPU): time vs number of threads (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 22; performance vs all cores), vs window size, separate
  vs fused operators, with and without writing output. Also a deliberately naive ``load the whole array'' NumPy version:
  it should run out of memory around 2\,m/1\,m, giving the report a real breaking point next to gdaldem's.
  \item E4 (GPU): time to copy to the GPU, kernel time and copy-back time measured separately with CUDA events; at which
  grid size the GPU starts to win; windows sized to fit the 8\,GB of GPU memory.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{What we expect (hypotheses to test):} if only computation is parallelised, at most $\sim$3.7$\times$ (Amdahl, since
read + write $\approx$ 27\%). With parallel decompression and fused operators the floor is set by the disk: reading the
16.6\,GB 1\,m file takes $\sim$8\,s at 2\,GB/s, versus 208\,s for gdaldem today. Writing a 43\,GB uncompressed output
would itself take $\sim$20\,s, so for display we will avoid writing at all. For the GPU, copying is cheap
($\sim$10\,GB/s), so the question is whether CPU-side reading/decompression can feed it fast enough.

\subsection{Phase 4: computing from the pyramid, and how wrong it is (E5, gap G2)}
\textbf{Method:} for each pyramid level $k$ (Chamoli 2\,m $\to$ 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\,m; tile 8\,m $\to$ 16--128\,m), compute
slope directly from that level using its true cell size $2^k \times$ base. As ``truth'', compute slope at full resolution
and average it onto the same coarse grid. Compare per cell: RMSE, mean bias (coarse slopes are expected to be
\emph{lower}, because averaging smooths the terrain), and the share of cells off by more than 5$^\circ$; split by slope
class (0--15, 15--30, 30--45, $>$45$^\circ$), since steep Himalayan terrain should suffer most. Record the time per level.
Secondary checks: does our 8\,m level from Chamoli match the independent HMA 8\,m DEM, and our 32\,m/64\,m levels the real
Copernicus 30\,m/90\,m? \textbf{Output:} an error-vs-time-saved curve: the headline research result. (Early hint: mean
slope was 28.8$^\circ$ at 90\,m, 31.1$^\circ$ at 30\,m and 32.5$^\circ$ at 8\,m, i.e.\ coarser grids flatten the terrain.)

\subsection{Phase 5: interactive viewer (E6)}
\textbf{Tile server} (\code{server/app.py}, FastAPI): a map client asks for tile $z/x/y$; the server converts it to a
ground area and resolution, picks the pyramid level whose cell size matches, reads that window plus a halo, computes
slope/aspect/hillshade with our kernel (CPU or GPU), colours it, and returns a PNG. Recently used tiles are kept in an
LRU cache. \textbf{Clients:} QGIS (as an XYZ tile layer) and a Cesium web globe (our tiles draped over the 3D terrain,
with a switch between products). \textbf{Measurements:} tile latency (median and 95th percentile) for (a) tiles
precomputed in advance, (b) on-the-fly CPU, (c) on-the-fly GPU; with and without cache; during a scripted pan/zoom
session. A demo video as backup for the final presentation.

\subsection{Phase 6 (stretch): viewshed and terrain distance}
Only if time allows. Viewshed: the exact R3 algorithm vs the faster approximate XDraw vs \code{gdal\_viewshed}, measuring
time and the share of cells classified differently from R3 (XDraw's accuracy loss in high relief is gap G3). Heights must
first be converted to one vertical datum (the 24.5\,m offset matters here). Terrain distance: Dijkstra vs A* on a
slope-based cost surface: time, nodes explored, same path.

with video fallback, code frozen and tagged.
